LinkedIn Keeps a Secret Credibility Score On You. Mine Is Certain I Want a Tesla.

Ira Wolfson — August 2026
Abstract: I asked LinkedIn for everything it knows about me. Buried in the export was a hidden scorecard grading my professional worth out of five, across twelve dimensions. It rated a conference talk and a newspaper feature at zero each — and my desire for a Tesla with more confidence than anything I have actually done.

Last month I gave a talk at SigmaPhi 2026, an international statistical-physics conference in Crete. This summer I published a result in Classical and Quantum Gravity: the factor of ¼ in a black hole’s entropy is not a fudge factor tuned to fit, but something forced by geometry itself. In July, a national newspaper ran a piece on it.

I mention all of this not to preen (well, maybe a bit!), but because I need you to hold it in your head for the next sixty seconds (but seriously — also to preen).

Unbeknownst to the protagonist of this story, LinkedIn has also been evaluating me. Quietly. It turns out the platform keeps a hidden credibility scorecard on you. I was bored on a bus ride, so I ordered a “complete” data export — the file you get when you ask LinkedIn for everything it knows about you. Apparently, it grades you, out of 5, across twelve dimensions of professional worth. Mine was last recomputed on July 20, eleven days after Crete, a week after the newspaper.

Here is what the machine concluded.

Academic contributions: 3. Institutional credibility: 3. Fair enough. Then it begins to descend. Notability: 2. Thought leadership: 2. Visibility: 2. Organizational influence: 1. Public reach: 1.

And then the floor drops out.

Event speaker: 0. Media recognition: 0. Honors: 0.

The talk in Crete: zero. The newspaper: zero. Fold all twelve dimensions together, and the aggregate reads 1.01 out of 5. Notability tier: 1 of 4. The bottom rung.

I could argue with the engine. I won’t. Because I’ll lose. I think it overestimated my academic contributions, and don’t get me started on thought leadership. The only thoughts I lead are my own (barely!).

To sum up, it told me I’m a freelancer (maybe, kind of, fine). It also thinks I’m “an active contributor who influences public opinion” — not sure I agree, but ok, I’ll take it. And it thinks, with more confidence than it assigned to any actual thing I have done, that I have an “affinity for electric vehicles.”

Which is, meh, I mean, I wouldn’t mind having a Tesla. But I also wouldn’t mind having Miles Davis’ original Martin Committee.

By the way, at some point I actually had a Martin Committee trumpet — its bell got smashed when I almost died, I fixed it with an old Yamaha bell and gave it to my cousin Yishai… but that’s another story.

So let us tally it honestly. Across twelve measures of my professional life, the algorithm scored me a zero, a one, or a two on nearly every one, and rated the whole of me at the lowest tier it offers. The single inference it got right — the one thing it read correctly about the person behind the profile — is that I would, in fact, not mind a Tesla.

LinkedIn: 0 for 12 on my career. 1 for 1 on my consumer desires.

I have never felt so precisely understood.
